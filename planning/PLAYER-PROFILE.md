# Player profile: the owner's taste, from the "About you" survey (answered 2026-10-08 to 2026-10-10, 198 survey answers plus 59 quick-idea answers and 29 follow-up comments; updated 2026-10-10)

Source: `planning/PLAYER-SURVEY.md` (the questions), answers read from the owner notes. Check every new idea round, game plan and agent brief against this file before building. It is a summary of what the owner said, not an interpretation beyond it; anything marked (unsure) is my reading. Open questions for them are the "Pq" items in `planning/FOR-YOU.md`. The foggy-forest dream game has its own notes in `planning/DREAM-GAME-PLAN.md`.

## What they love (play, watch, read)
- **Warframe (about 3,000 hours)** is the reference game: many ways to play inside one game (collector, character designer, loot and shoot, story, optimiser), fun alone and with others, easy to play half-asleep. Also Destiny 2, Portal 2 (favourite soundtrack, replayed for story nuance), Phasmophobia, Among Us (best gaming memory, banter with friends), Lifeline (branching choose-your-own-adventure), Flow Free, Duolingo, Universal Paperclips, the Duck Life games, The Stanley Parable (loved the humour and storytelling).
- **Genres:** puzzle and looter shooters first; sims and tycoons, horror, and watching city builders, idle games and strategy as well. Spatial and logic puzzles most, but all kinds. Phasmophobia's learn-more-get-easier investigation feel is a plus.
- **Best-feeling actions:** collecting and solving. They collect everything ("one of each item", every species), more than they collect cosmetics. Lore matters least unless it is a notebook being filled in.
- **100% feels good** when achievements are reachable and show how much of the game has been explored and understood. Best achievement kinds: exploration and completion.
- **Wants:** objectives without a strict order (three "get X to level 5 to unlock Z" goals visible at all times is the ideal), friendly welcome-back, intuitive games that need no tutorial, hard modes that are options and never the base game, easy-to-medium base difficulty.
- **Watches:** long commentary let's plays (2 hours or more, usually in bed) of 100% runs, glitch and build-breaking, horror and anomaly games, incrementals and colony games (worldbox, rimworld channels now); Ryan George and film or show reactions. Wants creators' "excited but calm, with humour" tone. Humour: dark and dry; absurd goes too far and "brainrot" references annoy them.
- **Reads and watches:** Marvel and science fiction (likes fiction that explains science that does not exist yet), dystopias, fanfiction with angst (skips long world-building). Fan of Tron's Rinzler and Spider-Man: tragic, quiet, broken-savior characters. Outside games: Ao3, YouTube, Instagram, Discord.

## What they dislike (do not build)
- **Roguelikes and deck or card games**: randomness that limits planning three steps ahead, and hard-lose states ("rng deciding if you die"). Luck is fine only as small multipliers, random events like Aftermath's, random spawn locations, and small surprises that keep an idle game interesting (Cookie Clicker, A Dark Room).
- **Timers, energy limits, grinding, losing progress** (hard fail only in an opt-in "ironman" mode). Timers over about 20 minutes or energy-to-play walls make them drop a game for good. Papers, Please's timer stress is "not my thing".
- Once-a-day mechanics that make something important unobtainable if missed (daily content is nice, daily pressure is not). They also say they personally cannot commit to daily games like Wordle.
- Visual novels, card-based games, RPGs that are only RPG; bright chaotic fantasy (gothic is fine); "obvious hero" characters who are all good and never make a mistake.
- Being the worst on a board: leaderboards must stay optional (they do not care about being best, but their friends do).
- Perfectionism turning a game into a job (they burn out). Mini Metro style games that add more of the same instead of new ideas bore them.
- Too much text at once ("a paragraph" is too much); icons-only buttons.

## How they play
- PC mostly, 10pm to 6am, often from bed; phone or Mac in class or half asleep. Sessions are **under 20 minutes or over 3 hours, nothing in between**. Friends mostly play on computers after class or work.
- Improviser who looks up a walkthrough or meta build when stuck (quits if under an hour invested). Predictability over surprises. Feels clever finding the one broken build (the YouTuber who finally uses the ignored chain lightning); proud completing a self-set session goal; stressed when their ability brings someone else down. Prefers helper or healer roles (Overwatch healer, CSGO meatshield); wants to create or help rather than compete.
- Short wins that build into a slow arc (many short missions that form a storyline; puzzles that form a bigger picture).
- Plays many games mainly because friends do (Among Us, CSGO, Overwatch, Dead by Daylight). Does not like sharing screenshots or share codes themselves.
- Plays their own site mostly to build a list of things to improve; favourite memory: grinding Canopy achievements. Most played now: Le Champ de Mots (French exam soon, French is "killing" them), SOL, Continuum (class, the only graded one). Has not played enough to say which game they are proudest of; says the BCM games "aren't really my style".
- Plays while doing other things when it is muscle memory (Warframe with YouTube) or turn-based with an auto mode (Star Wars Heroes in class).
- Mouse and keyboard both. Screens: iPhone 14 Pro, Mac about 14 inch, PC maybe 20 inch (they are unsure). Effects (particles, glow, shake) are liked but must be toggleable because their computer struggles.

## Mechanics in detail
- **Skill trees:** huge, many branches; they do not like a free-refund idea. Resources: about 3 at the start, more as the player learns; 20 plus is fine as a late-game thing.
- **Numbers:** huge exponential growth always feels good. Reward gaps: seconds to minutes ("3 minutes can feel like an hour").
- **Prestige/reset:** fine only if optional; slowing the pace to force a prestige is "dumb". **Offline progress:** liked, but not when the whole game is "leave it for an hour".
- **Hints:** only after asking ("would you like a suggestion?"), never suddenly highlighted; depends on how complex the game is. A graded hint ladder was a yes on the quick sheet (so the ladder must be opt-in).
- **Recipes:** handed to them, not discovered. **Optimising:** easy to get working, better with real depth, not spreadsheet level.
- **Openings:** start very simple, ramp up quickly so players who skipped the tutorial are not stuck on easy mode; they prefer working things out themselves.
- **Hidden achievements:** not fully hidden; a cryptic description is fine, not just "?". **Progress display:** a percentage bar, plus a shelf or sticker book for collections. Completion % on hub cards: yes. Per-game titles: dislike; account-wide titles could be great.
- **Quick-reaction games** are fun but they are unsure the site can hold real-time input.

## Failure and difficulty
- Failing should offer a way to restore progress (Warframe revive costing experience; temporary debuffs). Nothing permanent that cannot be got back "one way or another".
- Offer an easier mode every time or after about the 3rd failure, never forced. Several difficulties from the start in every game, not a hard mode unlocked after finishing. Difficulty is chosen by the player, never adaptive.
- Choices that stick are fine as long as progress can be restored. An "undo last decision" button was a no. An optional Ironman switch with its own badge was a yes.
- A very easy game is boring or relaxing depending on mood: both modes are wanted.

## Friends and community
- Realistically 1 friend plays SOL and 1 plays Continuum; getting friends to play is hard and the owner does not advertise. They would not push the site on others.
- Competitive friends like becoming so good nobody can compete, and being pushed when someone matches them. Nothing a competitive friend could do worries the owner, but they do not want to be seen as the worst: boards optional, with "hide my score".
- **Leaderboards per game**, not one for the hub (each game is its own skill). Friends leaving gifts or short notes (no chat): yes. Co-op where two friends fix one problem: yes (a puzzle game made to need teamwork, built after a solo puzzle game).
- Friends like first-person shooters, Jackbox and Valheim, which the site cannot do.
- Quick-sheet yes: friends-only boards with an invite code, hint gift, item gift, Keep Talking style two-player puzzle, shared station, community bar.

## Interface and look
- Dark, moody, earthy sometimes; any dark colour variants are hard to read on dark backgrounds, so contrast needs care (they asked for colour-theory-for-the-web notes for their assignment).
- Wants side panels to become overlay windows that open over the screen so PC mode feels like a PC game; their one good example is Warframe's mission progress view and menu. They did not understand "panels, wheel menus" (J10): explain with pictures.
- Buttons: one clear word, not icon-only, not many words. Text short, bullets outside collapsible extra info.
- Pictures and scenes over numbers and charts to show how they are doing (S18), yet a percentage bar is the baseline progress display. Short animation over a log entry (S7). Colour per game as fits the mood; Canopy, SOL, Continuum are not "foggy" games, Champ's fading memory fog is the one exception.
- Audio: wants clicks, soundtrack, alerts and ambience all at once as a one to two week block when it happens (the date is unsure). Portal 2 is the soundtrack benchmark.
- Landscape/trophy ideas: completing Canopy, Tide and others could grow a landscape with more trees or deeper water by completion % (C7 comment).

## Story
- Only Lifeline style, with lots of branching and optional unless it is the whole game. Short missions that join into a story.
- Their sketch: you are someone with radios who gets a weird signal from an unknown person and must earn their trust; maybe a reveal that they are a parallel version of you. The predicament is undecided.
- Companion: a robot that barely speaks but shows emotion (like the droid in Maul). Log and notes jokes: dry and dark.
- Limits: the character can be in a rough place, but no self-harm and no self-hatred spirals; never joke about harmful mental-health actions (eating disorders, depression behaviours).
- Okay with a sequel and recurring crew (they would not be against it). Real-world games: show multiple points of view.

## Themes and tone
- **Themes:** space, computers, robots, science fiction; the ocean only when creepy or dark. Wants teaching games to continue but "more game than teaching" once the teaching core exists (already heavy on climate, languages, history). New subject interests: coding, maths, AI, chemistry, physics; any topic, start simple. Caring topics: food, healthcare, water, housing, body autonomy, LGBTQ, women's and racial rights.
- **Look:** low-poly is a safe default, hand-drawn on occasion, pixel art only if the game makes sense with it; they will make rough graphics if needed.
- **Facts:** sources easy to find but not always shown on screen; "fun that happens to be accurate" over "a game that explains the real world".
- **Audience:** them and their friends now, strangers later. First minute: "no matter who you are there is a game for you". Accessible to everyone (the owner has no needs themselves). Three words: diverse, rewarding, engaging.

## This or that results (S section; 1 or 2 as answered)

Finish-once-and-keep versus grows-forever: both.

## School and portfolio
- Majors: international history, digital and social media; personal interests: computers, maths, chemistry, physics. Only Continuum is graded now and it is the process that is graded. Dev logs are a general dump and can become something more formal. French is the subject a game could teach best.

## How they want me to work
- After an ideas list is done, "you decide" means use judgement. Small spec tweaks (10% to 15% because it makes more sense) are mine to just do unless they stated the number themselves, not as "something like 20%". A surprise is only when they approve something without specifics.
- When unsure whether something fits their taste: **ask, and give safe alternative options**. Open input goes in FOR-YOU and I skip it meanwhile; nothing else needs checking first.
- Ideas sheets: **many quick items is good**; scale the number of questions to how finished a game is; if something applies to **more than 4 games, put it in the cross-game section**. Ask a question as a short yes/no item they can answer from their phone, not "answer this in a file".
- Summaries: when the usage limit resets and when they ask. Biggest past annoyance: forgetting to update logs or the coordination board.
- Rhythm: they build Saturday to Monday until usage runs out, answer questions mid-week, and soon playtest Thursday and Friday. Feedback: a notes box on the ideas sheet with a game dropdown that remembers the last game used (today they paste a long list from their notes).
- If agents have barely done anything after several resets, check why; slow tests are their CPU, not a problem to fix.
