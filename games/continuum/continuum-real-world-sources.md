# Continuum — Real-World Sources by Era

**Purpose:** feeds the collapsed "Info" panel described in `CLAUDE.md` — one panel, updated with the current era's sources as the player progresses, never shown on the main screen unprompted. Same rule as the climate quartet's research docs: this is reference material to draw from, not text to dump wholesale into the game.

All sources verified via live web search. Given how many of these are historical/archaeological rather than current data, expect fewer of these to go stale than the climate quartet's sources did — but still worth a spot-check before final submission.

---

## Tribal

1. **Pressbooks Nebraska — "Political Systems" (Introduction to Anthropology)**
 https://pressbooks.nebraska.edu/anth110/chapter/political-systems/
 *Relation:* explains the real anthropological distinction between bands and tribes, and how village growth and rectangular architecture marked the shift — useful for grounding the Tribal era's population/settlement mechanics in real social-organization stages.

2. **UNESCO World Heritage Centre — Neolithic Site of Çatalhöyük**
 https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1405/
 *Relation:* one of the earliest known settlements, notable for its egalitarian layout (comparably-sized dwellings, no social hierarchy visible in the architecture) — a strong real-world anchor for a Tribal era built around communal, non-hierarchical starting conditions.

3. **PMC — "The study of early human settlement preference and settlement prediction in Xinjiang, China"**
 https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8948180/
 *Relation:* academic treatment of how early humans chose settlement locations based on geography and environment — useful if the Tribal era's map/placement mechanics want a real methodological basis.

4. **Research Outreach / Medium — Dunbar's Number**
 https://researchoutreach.org/articles/size-matters-social-groups-human-evolution/
 *Relation:* the real cognitive/social limit on stable group size (~150, in a layered structure of 5/15/50/150/500/1500) — directly useful for calibrating Tribal-era population thresholds where social structure would realistically need to change.

## Agrarian

1. **National Geographic — "What was the Neolithic Revolution?"**
 https://www.nationalgeographic.com/culture/article/neolithic-agricultural-revolution
 *Relation:* explains how early farmers produced storable surplus, directly relevant to the Agrarian era's resource-storage and surplus mechanics.

2. **HISTORY — "Neolithic Revolution"**
 https://www.history.com/articles/neolithic-revolution
 *Relation:* accessible overview of the shift from nomadic to settled farming life, with Çatalhöyük as the bridge example from Tribal — good for the era-transition narrative beat specifically.

3. **EBSCO Research Starters — "Neolithic Revolution"**
 https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/agriculture-and-agribusiness/neolithic-revolution
 *Relation:* covers the emergence of specialized tools, pottery, and early urbanization as direct consequences of food surplus — useful for research-tree node justification in this era.

4. **TxWes Pressbooks — "The Neolithic Revolution" (World History)**
 https://txwes.pressbooks.pub/internationalstudies/chapter/2-3-the-neolithic-revolution-world-history-volume-1-to-1500-openstax/
 *Relation:* explains how surplus specifically created the first social hierarchies and property accumulation — a real-world basis if the sustainability score wants to weigh equity, not just output.

## Classical

1. **The Getty — "Mesopotamia: Civilization Begins"**
 https://www.getty.edu/art/exhibitions/mesopotamia/explore.html
 *Relation:* describes Uruk's growth into a walled city of ~100,000 via intensive irrigation agriculture directed by temple estates — a real model for the Classical era's shift from agrarian surplus to coordinated civic infrastructure.

2. **TheCollector — "What Can Uruk and Ur Teach Us About the Birth of Civilization?"**
 https://www.thecollector.com/uruk-and-ur-birth-of-civilization/
 *Relation:* details how canal management required centralized planning and coordinated labor — a strong real basis for introducing "civic institution" mechanics in this era.

3. **PLOS One — "Morphodynamic Foundations of Sumer"**
 https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0329084
 *Relation:* recent research proposing that natural tidal irrigation, not just human ingenuity, jumpstarted Sumerian urbanization — a nuance worth reflecting if the era wants to credit environment as well as player choices.

4. **GlobalSecurity.org — "Uruk Period"**
 https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/world/iraq/history-uruk.htm
 *Relation:* gives concrete scale (population ~45,000, a trade network stretching from Turkey to Iran) — useful reference numbers for calibrating Classical-era city scale.

## Medieval

1. **Popular Archaeology — "Inside a Medieval City: Public Health, Housing, and Everyday Regulations"**
 https://popular-archaeology.com/article/inside-a-medieval-city-public-health-housing-and-everyday-regulations/
 *Relation:* real medieval sanitation regulation (waste fines, zoning of toxic trades away from drinking water) — good grounding for a Medieval-era livability mechanic tied to sanitation/zoning choices.

2. **SAGE Journals — Coomans & Hermenault, "Public Works, Spatial Strategies, and Mobility in Late Medieval Ghent"**
 https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00961442221124892
 *Relation:* academic analysis of how medieval authorities used public-works investment specifically to mitigate risk (floods, disease, military vulnerability) — a real precedent for tying infrastructure spending to resilience, not just growth.

3. **Johns Hopkins University Press — Magnusson, "Urban Infrastructure in Medieval England: Sustainability and Resilience"**
 https://press.jhu.edu/books/title/12618/urban-infrastructure-medieval-england
 *Relation:* a recent academic study literally framed around sustainability and resilience in medieval public works — directly useful as the Medieval era's clearest bridge to the game's SDG 11 framing.

4. **Fiveable — "Urban Revival and the Growth of Cities" (European History 1000–1500)**
 https://fiveable.me/europe-1000-1500/unit-7/urban-revival-growth-cities/study-guide/91Ey4PdQIbJK9yOx
 *Relation:* explains how guild specialization and municipal charters granted real self-governance — good basis for a "civic autonomy" research-tree branch in this era.

## Industrial

1. **Britannica — "Urban Planning: Industrialization, Infrastructure, Cities"**
 https://www.britannica.com/topic/urban-planning/The-era-of-industrialization
 *Relation:* describes how 19th-century industrial growth outpaced planning, producing slums and disease before sanitation reform emerged — the clearest real precedent for an Industrial era built around a genuine growth-vs-livability crisis.

2. **The Economic Journal (Oxford Academic) — "Coal Smoke, City Growth, and the Costs of the Industrial Revolution"**
 https://academic.oup.com/ej/article-abstract/130/626/462/5602598
 *Relation:* rigorous economic research showing industrial pollution measurably reduced long-run city growth — directly useful if the Industrial era wants its livability score to have a real, provable growth cost, not just a moral one.

3. **Sociology.Institute — "The Evolution of Industrial Cities"**
 https://sociology.institute/urban-sociology/evolution-industrial-cities-work-urban-environment/
 *Relation:* gives the striking real statistic that global urban population rose from 3% (1800) to nearly 50% (2000) — a concrete number worth reflecting somewhere in the era's scale or transition beat.

4. **PMC — "Bibliometric Analysis and Research Trend Forecast of Healthy Urban Planning for 40 Years"**
 https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8464861/
 *Relation:* traces how modern urban planning as a discipline emerged directly in response to industrial-era public health crises — a good real link from this era into the Current/Digital era's more formalized SDG 11 framing.

## Current/Digital

1. **UN DESA — "SDG 11: Sustainable Cities and Communities"**
 https://desapublications.un.org/publications/sdgs/SDG%2011%20Sustainable%20Cities%20and%20Communities
 *Relation:* the official UN publication on this goal — the most direct, authoritative source for the era the whole game's SDG framing is built around.

2. **Our World in Data — "Make Cities Inclusive, Safe, Resilient and Sustainable"**
 https://ourworldindata.org/sdgs/sustainable-cities
 *Relation:* breaks SDG 11 into its actual 10 targets and 16 measurable indicators — useful if the Current/Digital era's sustainability score wants to reflect specific, real dimensions (housing, transport, green space) rather than one abstract number.

3. **National Geographic Education — "Sustainable Development Goal 11: Sustainable Cities and Communities"**
 https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/sustainable-development-goal-11-sustainable-cities-and-communities/
 *Relation:* notes that between 2000 and 2014, urban areas grew 1.28 times faster than their populations — i.e., cities are sprawling and getting less dense — a real, usable tension for this era's zoning/density mechanics.

4. **Agenda 2030 in Latin America and the Caribbean (ECLAC) — "Sustainable Cities and Communities"**
 https://agenda2030lac.org/en/sdg/11-sustainable-cities-and-communities
 *Relation:* concrete present-day figures (70% of people projected to live in cities by 2050; 1.1 billion currently in slum or slum-like conditions) — strong, current numbers for the info panel to cite directly.

### Current Day (bonus — dedicated "right now" source, distinct from the era's general SDG 11 grounding above)

**UN — The Sustainable Development Goals Report 2025**
https://desapublications.un.org/publications/sdgs/SDG%2011%20Sustainable%20Cities%20and%20Communities
*Relation:* the tenth annual global stocktaking of SDG progress, delivering an explicitly present-moment assessment: real gains made, but current pace insufficient to meet 2030 targets. Worth surfacing as the one source that answers "where do things actually stand right now," separate from the era's broader historical/definitional sources above.

## Space Age

1. **Big Think — "Urban planning in space: 3 off-world designs for future cities"**
 https://bigthink.com/hard-science/urban-planning-in-space/
 *Relation:* covers three real design concepts (NASA's 1977 rotating habitat study, MIT's Mars City "Redwood Forest" competition winner, ring habitats) — a good overview source for grounding the Space Age era's variety of settlement types.

2. **Planetizen — "NASA's Urban Plan for Space Settlements"**
 https://www.planetizen.com/news/2018/12/101790-nasas-urban-plan-space-settlements
 *Relation:* frames NASA's 1977 "Space Settlements: A Design Study" as literally a city-planning policy document (residential space, schools, transportation infrastructure) — the clearest real precedent that space settlement design is genuinely continuous with ordinary urban planning, not a total departure from it.

3. **Columbia University Press — Scharmen, "Space Settlements"**
 https://cup.columbia.edu/book/space-settlements/9781941332498/
 *Relation:* an academic architectural history treating NASA's 1975 Summer Study designs as serious speculative architecture — useful if the Space Age era wants a citation for why "future city" designs are worth taking seriously as real design problems, not just science fiction.

4. **Dagstuhl / SpaceCHI 2025 — "Designing for Usability in Modular Space Habitats: A Space Syntax Perspective"**
 https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/01oasics/oasics-vol130-spacechi2025/OASIcs.SpaceCHI.2025.4/OASIcs.SpaceCHI.2025.4.pdf
 *Relation:* current (2025) academic research on how modular space habitat layouts affect real usability at scale — a contemporary, technical source if the Space Age era's infrastructure/zoning mechanics want a genuinely up-to-date basis rather than relying solely on the 1970s NASA study.
